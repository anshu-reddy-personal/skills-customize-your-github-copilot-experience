About Leave No Trace

Leave No Trace (LNT) is a set of outdoor ethics developed by the Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics to promote responsible enjoyment of the natural world. The seven principles below apply to all visits to national parks, wilderness areas, and public lands — from a short day hike to a multi-week backcountry expedition.

Every year, increasing park visitation creates measurable impacts on soil, vegetation, water quality, and wildlife behavior. Following these principles preserves the experiences that make national parks worth visiting.

1 Plan Ahead and Prepare

Poor planning leads to safety hazards, rule violations, and environmental damage. Research conditions, regulations, and required permits before you go.

  • Check weather forecasts and park alerts the day before your visit
  • Know if your destination requires a permit or timed-entry reservation
  • Download offline maps — cell service is unreliable in most parks
  • Tell someone your itinerary and expected return time
  • Repackage food in reusable containers to reduce waste
  • Learn about fire regulations — campfires are restricted or banned in many areas

2 Travel and Camp on Durable Surfaces

Repeated foot traffic compacts soil, kills plants, and creates new "social trails" that fragment habitat. Concentrate use on already-impacted surfaces.

  • Stay on designated trails — do not shortcut switchbacks
  • In popular areas, always walk in the center of the established trail
  • In pristine backcountry, spread out to avoid creating new paths
  • Camp on durable surfaces: bare rock, gravel, sand, or established sites
  • Camp at least 200 feet (about 70 adult paces) from lakes and streams
  • Cryptobiotic soil crust — the black, lumpy crust in desert parks is alive; one footstep can take 50–250 years to recover. Walk on rock or sand only.

3 Dispose of Waste Properly

"Pack it in, pack it out" applies to all trash, leftover food, and gear. Human waste management is critical in high-use areas.

  • Pack out all trash including food scraps, fruit peels, and nut shells (they don't decompose quickly and attract wildlife)
  • For solid human waste: dig a cathole 6–8 inches deep, 200 feet from water, trails, and campsites; pack out used toilet paper or use a WAG bag where required
  • Dispose of gray water (dishwater) 200 feet from water sources; strain food particles and pack them out
  • Use biodegradable soap only and only 200+ feet from water
  • Pick up micro-trash — small wrappers and pieces of gear others left behind

4 Leave What You Find

National parks are protected by federal law. Removing natural objects — rocks, plants, antlers, artifacts — is illegal and deprives others of the same discovery.

  • Do not pick wildflowers, remove rocks, or collect anything from the park
  • Leave natural objects where you find them — this includes "pretty" rocks and shed antlers
  • Do not disturb cultural artifacts, rock art, or historic structures
  • Avoid introducing or transporting non-native species — clean boots between parks
  • Do not build cairns, rock stacks, or create other human structures; they confuse navigation and disturb wildlife habitat

5 Minimize Campfire Impacts

Campfires cause lasting impacts, risk wildfire, and are prohibited in many parks and wilderness areas. Use a camp stove whenever possible.

  • Check current fire restrictions before building any fire
  • Use a camp stove for cooking — it is lighter, faster, and has zero fire risk
  • Where fires are permitted, use established fire rings and keep fires small
  • Burn only dead and downed wood no larger than your wrist; never cut live wood
  • Allow wood to burn completely to ash; drown fires with water and stir until cool to the touch
  • Never leave a fire unattended

6 Respect Wildlife

Wildlife that becomes habituated to humans — through feeding, close approach, or campsite raids — often has to be euthanized. Your behavior directly affects their survival.

  • Stay at least 100 yards (91 m) from bears and wolves
  • Stay at least 25 yards (23 m) from all other wildlife including bison, elk, and deer
  • Never feed wildlife — "a fed bear is a dead bear" applies to all animals
  • Store food, trash, and scented items in bear canisters or bear boxes at all times in camp
  • Do not make sudden movements or sounds around wildlife; observe quietly
  • Keep dogs leashed at all times and on designated trails only
  • Avoid wildlife during sensitive seasons: denning, nesting, raising young, and mating

7 Be Considerate of Other Visitors

Everyone comes to national parks to experience nature. Noise, overcrowding popular spots, and inconsiderate behavior diminish the experience for everyone.

  • Yield the trail: hikers going uphill have the right of way; all hikers yield to horses
  • Keep noise levels low — voices carry far in quiet wilderness settings
  • Use headphones for music; many visitors come specifically for the sounds of nature
  • Move off-trail (on durable surfaces) to let faster hikers pass
  • At viewpoints and popular spots, take your photo and move on — don't monopolize the space
  • Drone use is prohibited in all national parks without a special use permit
  • Respect the solitude of fellow campers — quiet hours in campgrounds are typically 10 PM–6 AM

📣 Spread the Word

The best way to protect national parks is to share LNT principles with fellow visitors. Be a positive example on the trail. If you see someone causing harm, a friendly, non-confrontational reminder goes a long way.

Learn more at lnt.org and the NPS Leave No Trace page.
